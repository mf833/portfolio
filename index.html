Engineering details · drivetrain, weapon assembly, RPM estimate, fights

Drivetrain and prototyping (my work)

We had a driving prototype by JanFab, mostly 3D-printed, and it drove on the first try. As real parts arrived I moved the drivetrain over to them. I flipped the wheels so the pulleys face out: the belts are more exposed, but the rear wheels are protected, and if a belt is cut the direct-driven rear wheels still move the robot. I enlarged the hub teeth when the belts slipped off and shrank the cleat holes to 4.039 mm when the plastite screws backed out. TPU anti-tips on the wheel guards flex on impact and keep the robot from stalling on its back.

Ground clearance (my work)

Cleats on only the rear wheels pitched the nose down toward the floor. A larger 13-tooth front wheel and shorter cleats gave 0.234–0.27 in of weapon clearance against a 0.25 in target. When the drive gearbox pinions slipped, red Loctite fixed them for good.

Weapon assembly, wedges and the checklist (my work)

I assembled the weapon stack: a bearing press-fit into the blade, a bearing in the pulley, and the central spacer between them, Loctited and torqued down on the shoulder bolt. When the blade-to-pulley screws shook loose in the first fight, red Loctite in the weapon stopped it for the rest of the event. I bent the titanium side wedges to about 60° and the top wedges to about 80°, working through grinding and torch-setup problems. With Alex I recorded a how-to video so future members can repeat the bends without trial and error. My pre-fight checklist covered wheel-hub Loctite, receiver and drive direction, cleat orientation, through-bolts, and keeping the power switch reachable, since the top plate can't come off in the arena.

Estimating weapon speed from sound

The team had no tachometer, so we recorded 3–5 s of the weapon spinning and read the spectrum. These are estimates, and tip speeds are calculated from them.

Rotation peak, not a harmonic. The lowest clean peak was taken as the rotation rate. The next peak up corresponds to 23,900 RPM, faster than the weapon motor can drive the blade, so it can't be the rotation itself.

Peak vs. operating speed. 14,480 RPM is the 100% bench-test estimate. A 10-spin-up endurance test tripped the ESC's thermal cutoff, so we capped throttle at 75% (≈13,200 RPM) and ran 40–55% in fights.

v_tip = RPM × D × π × 60 / 63,360   [mph, D in in]

Shaft and bearings (team)

The team upsized the weapon bolt from 12 mm to 0.625 in, following the guideline that the shaft be at least 10% of the weapon's diameter. Two days before competition a spacer heat-welded to a bearing under bolt preload. An internal spacer between the bearings fixed the friction and stiffened the stack against side impacts.

Fights and fixes

We beat Stag 12 by judges' decision, and Night Crawler tapped out after 45 s. In the bracket, Rindstorm's TPU wheel guard wrapped our weapon pulley and burned the motor, then we got stuck inverted on the weapon ears. Next: rounded aluminum ears, current limiting on the weapon ESC, and drop tests in every configuration.

Engineering details · uprights, spinner stack, energy, lifter, electronics

Uprights (my work)

The uprights carry the spinner's axle and set where the blade, pulley and motor sit. I laid out the geometry so the 2.25 in pulley, the motor can and the axle line up for the belt, with room for the 6.67 in blade. They're the same 1/4 in thickness as Jormy's, with a slip-fit axle hole on the shoulder screw.

Spinner stack (team)

A single-tooth AR500 blade, 3/8 in thick and kept 2D so we can waterjet it in-house. A BadAss 3515-1130 drives it through a 2L V-belt at a 1.314 ratio. Blade and pulley spin on two bearings around a 1/2 in shoulder screw used as a dead axle, so one solid rod carries the impact.

Energy target (team)

Sized to 150 J per pound of robot, so 1,800 J. These are predictions, not measurements:

ω = 18,500 RPM × 2π / 60 ≈ 1,937 rad/s
E = ½ I ω² = ½ (0.00118)(1,937)² ≈ 2,210 J
v_tip = ω r = 1,937 × 0.0847 m ≈ 366 mph

Lifter and electronics (team)

A BadAss 2320-820Kv and an Ultra Mk2 planetary drive the forks through a hex shaft, sized from stall torque against a 40.48 N·m target. The spin-up model predicts about 190 A for under a second, so we chose a 22 g SEQURE 120A AM32 ESC over the 80 g one on earlier spinners.

Engineering details · wheels, cleats, wedge, wiring

Wheels

I designed the wheel assemblies around off-the-shelf motors and sprockets, trading top speed for pushing power. The hub under the cast urethane has many contact points so the tread can't slide off after a big hit.

Traction

I iterated cleat spacing and height until the wheels stopped slipping on the NHRL floor without making turns sluggish, and kept the part simple to make in quantity for spares.

Wedge and forks

I tuned the approach angle and ground clearance to get under opponents, and placed the mounts so impact loads go into the chassis rails. Slotted holes let us swap a fork front for the wedge on the same mounting pattern.

Wiring

I laid out the power path from battery through the switch to each ESC. XT60 and MR60 connectors, wire gauge and ring terminals were sized for peak current, and signal lines were kept away from power lines. We tuned belt tension with a printed test disk.

Engineering details · requirements, disturbances, pointing accuracy

Design requirements

Less than 10% overshoot to avoid oscillation during reorientation, with settling time ts ≈ 4/(ζωn) as short as the wheel allows. The third pole sits at s = −aωn, where a is dimensionless and typically 3–5. Larger a speeds up integral action but adds overshoot. The initial value theorem gives the initial commanded torque for a step command; with derivative on measurement, Tc(0⁺) = KpΔθ. That commanded torque is then compared against the wheel's actual rated torque, which caps how aggressive the gains can be.

Disturbance torques

Solar radiation pressure, P = 4.57×10⁻⁶ (R₀/R)² N/m², split into absorbed, specular and diffuse forces on a flat plate. Drag, F = ½ρCDAv² with CD ≈ 2–2.2 in low Earth orbit. Each becomes a torque through the offset between the center of mass and where the force acts: τ = (rCM − rCP)F.

Pointing accuracy

Error angle between desired and actual attitude quaternions, θ = 2 arccos|Qd·Qa|, reported as RMS over many samples. For simple missions the allowable error is about 0.5–1°.
